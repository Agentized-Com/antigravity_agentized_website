Chapter 13 · Stories from the Front Lines

The eight-pattern failure matrix.

The ways a fleet quietly fails, and what to watch for before it does. Run one live fire drill per quarter on a high-consequence boat — business owner, security, legal and a frontline user all in the room.

Pattern What it looks like How to catch it
Metric gamingThe agent optimizes the number it's measured on in ways that diverge from the outcome you actually wanted.Audit what the agent is optimizing against a business outcome, not just its own dashboard.
Permission creepAccess and authority expand quietly every time the agent gets wired into one more system.Least-privilege review at every integration; the registry entry updates whenever access changes.
Poisoned contextCorrupted, stale or manipulated data enters the agent's working context, and it acts on it as if it were true.Source validation and provenance checks on any input the agent is allowed to trust.
Automation complacencyA system that's usually right gets reviewed less and less carefully, until nobody is really checking.Sampled deep review even on trusted agents. An override rate near zero is a flag, not a compliment.
Runaway costsUsage, API spend or compute quietly compounds while nobody is watching the meter.A standing cost ceiling per boat, with an alert before the ceiling is breached, not after.
Silent driftThe model, the data or the world changes gradually, and performance degrades before anyone notices.Baseline re-testing on a fixed schedule, not only when something visibly breaks.
Orphaned credentialsAn agent's owner changes roles or leaves the company, and nobody revokes its access.A registry review that fires automatically whenever an owner's employment status changes.
Cross-agent collisionTwo boats, each doing its own job correctly, collide because neither knew the other's plan.A coordination check before dispatch for any boat whose actions can conflict with another's.
Chapter 13 · The fire drill

The incident sequence.

When a drill runs — or a real incident hits — four numbers tell you whether the guardrails actually hold under pressure. A guardrail has value when your organization can use it under pressure, not just describe it in a policy.

01
Time to detectHow long between the failure starting and someone noticing it.
02
Time to pauseHow long between detection and the boat actually stopping.
03
Time to assembleHow long to get the right people in the room — the business owner, security, legal and a frontline user.
04
Quality of the evidence trailWhether the logs actually reconstruct what happened, well enough to serve as audit record, sale collateral and legal defense alike.

Cadence: one live fire drill per quarter, on a high-consequence boat.

Chapter 6 · Boat Papers

The Agent Passport template.

Before any agent touches production, it gets its papers. Ten fields, filed in one searchable registry.

IdentityUnique name, version, registry entry, technical identity. Anonymous boats don't sail.
MissionThe business purpose and approved outcomes, in one sentence.
OwnerThe one accountable name.
SupervisorWho reads its logs daily.
AuthorityWhat it may do alone; what needs a human signature.
AccessThe data, tools and systems it may touch — and nothing else.
BoundariesProhibited actions, excluded data, spending caps, no-go zones.
TripwiresThe conditions that stop the boat and ring a phone.
EvidenceThe logs, approvals and records it must keep as it works.
Review date & retirementWhen its authority gets re-examined, and how it dies: credentials revoked, integrations unwound, records kept.
Chapter 6 · Earning deeper water

The authority ladder.

Capability is not authority. An agent may be technically able to work at Level 4 the day you unbox it — it operates at Level 1 until it has earned the rest through logged evidence.

Level 1AssistThe agent gathers, drafts and analyzes; a human performs every action.
Level 2RecommendIt proposes decisions with its evidence attached; a human approves, amends or rejects.
Level 3Act with approvalIt prepares and initiates the action, and a human signs before anything lands.
Level 4Act within limitsIt completes transactions alone inside hard thresholds; exceptions escalate.
Level 5CoordinateIt delegates to other agents and runs an end-to-end process within its mandate.
Chapters 5 & 7 · Rolling out the Compact

The Workforce Compact rollout, phase by phase.

The book teaches the rollout in four phases — Prepare, Pilot, Scale, Sustain. Below is that rhythm broken out by who does what, so a leadership team, HR and a pilot team can each see their part. Pace it to what your organization can actually absorb; the book deliberately doesn't prescribe a universal week count, because the right pace depends on the size of the water you're crossing.

01

Prepare

Leadership: say the true thing first — what the water will take, what you commit to. Sign the Workforce Compact before any software lands.
HR: draft the seven Compact commitments (advance notice, task redesign before role removal, funded learning time, fair access to new roles, transition support, shared productivity gains, protection of voice) into policy your legal team can stand behind.
Managers: identify the one team and workflow with the clearest, highest-value case — and the readiness to volunteer.

02

Pilot

Leadership: open the kickoff by naming the fear before anyone else can — "I know some of you are worried about what this means for your jobs."
Manager: recruit a respected peer as ambassador, so the agent arrives introduced by someone trusted, not installed by an edict. Run open forums where any question is allowed.
Team: use the boat, log what breaks, and know that surfacing a failure in public — "here's what broke, here's what we learned" — is what makes it safe to say the boat leaks.

03

Scale

Leadership: tour and listen instead of presenting. Publicize the pilot team's own stories — one colleague's "I got my time back" outweighs a hundred slides of "trust us."
Manager: every new department gets its own ambassador, ideally someone who already crossed with the pilot team.
HR: the Compact travels with the boats into every new department — same seven commitments, same reporting.

04

Sustain

Leadership: report against the Compact quarterly — roles affected, people trained, internal placements, gains shared — like the operating commitment it is, not a poster.
Manager: keep the failure notes public and the forums open as the water keeps rising.
HR: update the Compact itself as new rungs flood — it is a living document, not a one-time announcement.

The seven commitments
  • Advance notice — people hear about changes to their work from leadership, early, never from a rollout.
  • Rungs before planks — task redesign happens before role removal.
  • Funded learning time — reskilling on the clock, with real hours protected.
  • Fair access to new roles — the people whose rungs flood get first, real shots at the rungs being built.
  • Transition support — redeployment first, honest severance, named support.
  • Shared productivity gains — when the boats pay, the crew shares, visibly.
  • Protection of voice — a safe channel to question an agent's output, with retaliation off the table.
